Buying & Selling Ejido Land in Baja California Sur

Understand what is being offered, what paperwork exists and which steps apply before you buy or sell. A practical starting point for land in Los Cabos and nearby Pacific and East Cape areas.

Start with the land’s legal status

“Ejido land” can describe different situations. The name of a community, a map pin or an MLS description does not establish the legal status of a particular parcel. Start with the exact documents and current registry information.

Still under the ejidal regime

Identify whether the interest concerns an allocated parcel, common-use land or a settlement lot. These do not all follow the same transfer route. A parcel certificate records agrarian rights; it is not private-property title. [1]

Dominio pleno is in progress

Assembly authorization is a step, not proof that a parcel is already private property. For an eligible parcel, the individual title process involves the RAN and subsequent Public Property Registry recording. Ask for evidence of the actual stage reached. [3]

Former ejido land with private title

Review the issued title, the exit from the agrarian register, Public Property Registry information and the subsequent chain of ownership. The first sale after conversion can still involve preference-right formalities. [7]

Buying ejido or former ejido land: a practical process

Use these steps to organize the investigation. The transaction route must be determined for the particular land and the people involved.

  1. Define the property and your intended use

    Identify the ejido, parcel or lot number, municipality, approximate area and exact location. Explain whether you want a personal home, development site, agricultural use or another project. Confirm that the land shown on the visit is the land described in the paperwork.

  2. Check the current holder and records

    Request the relevant certificate or title and current registry evidence. Ask an agrarian attorney to review who holds the rights, the seller’s authority and any succession or competing claim. RAN registration and rights-status certificates are useful parts of that review. [4]

  3. Confirm that the proposed buyer and transfer qualify

    For a sale of parcel rights under Article 80, the statutory route is to another ejidatario or recognized avecindado of the same ejido. Mexican nationality alone is not enough. Have the buyer’s actual eligibility checked before choosing a contract or paying a deposit. [2]

  4. Investigate the physical land and project

    Compare the survey, boundaries and measured area. Check legal and physical access, occupation, water, electricity, topography and planned use with the relevant professionals and authorities. Ownership status and permission to build are separate questions.

  5. Set written conditions before money changes hands

    Ask the reviewing professionals to identify outstanding documents, notices, filings and any conversion steps. The agreement should address who performs and pays for each step, payment release conditions, deadlines and what happens if a required condition cannot be met. Obtain advice on the appropriate payment arrangements.

  6. Complete the applicable formalities and verify the result

    A parcel-rights transfer involves a written agreement with witnesses, ratification before a public certifying officer, applicable preference-right notices, notice to the comisariado and RAN registration. Private-title transactions follow their own closing and registration route. Keep final documents and verify completion rather than relying only on a filing receipt. [5]

Can foreigners buy ejido land?

A foreign buyer cannot acquire ejidatario or avecindado status under the nationality requirements in the Agrarian Law. Buying former ejido land after a valid conversion to private property is a different transaction. [1]

For privately titled property in the restricted coastal zone, foreign buyers must also address Mexico’s foreign-ownership rules, commonly through an authorized bank trust (fideicomiso) for residential property. A trust does not itself convert ejido rights into private title. Have the attorney, notario and bank confirm the route for your case. [6]

Selling ejido or former ejido land: prepare the transaction

A well-organized property file helps identify the buyers you can approach and the conditions you can actually offer.

  1. Establish exactly what you hold

    Gather the parcel certificate, common-use certificate, title or other documents relevant to your case. Identify all holders and any representative or succession issue. Describe the interest being offered accurately; do not advertise an expected future title as though it already exists.

  2. Resolve gaps before setting a firm closing promise

    Ask for a review of names, parcel numbers, maps, boundaries, area and registry history. List what is missing, who must supply it and whether proceedings or competing claims need resolution. An unresolved file changes the transaction timetable.

  3. Confirm the buyer group and transaction route

    Determine whether the proposed transaction concerns agrarian rights, private property, a lease or another arrangement. Ask who can legally acquire the particular interest. If conversion is contemplated, obtain case-specific advice on eligibility and outstanding steps; do not promise that every parcel can be converted.

  4. Plan for notices and preference rights

    Ask which people have preference rights and how notice or a valid waiver must be documented. The rules for a parcel-rights sale and for a first sale after dominio pleno are different. These formalities should be built into the transaction plan, not left until the last moment. [7]

  5. Price the interest and agree the conditions

    Compare genuinely similar transactions and the status of their paperwork, access and services. Document what the price includes, the payment currency, who pays transaction expenses and any conditions still to be completed. Ask for a written tax and cost estimate for the specific seller and transaction.

  6. Track completion and preserve the file

    Coordinate signing, payment conditions, applicable registration and possession arrangements with the professionals handling the matter. Keep copies of notices, receipts, signed documents and final registry evidence. Agree how any remaining practical obligations will be handled.

Documents and information to assemble

The list varies with the transaction. Start by identifying what exists; the reviewing professional should specify the complete file and which originals or certified copies are needed.

Identity, authority and rights

  • Names of the current holder or holders and their role.
  • Certificate, title or other document describing the interest.
  • Relevant authority to act, succession documents and assembly records, where applicable.
  • Current registry evidence and information about known claims or proceedings.

The parcel and its practical condition

  • Ejido, parcel or lot number, location and municipality.
  • Plan or survey, boundaries and recorded versus measured area.
  • Access, occupation, leases or other arrangements affecting use.
  • Available information on services, permitted use and project restrictions.

The proposed transaction

  • Intended buyer or seller role and the interest to be transferred.
  • Preference-right notices or waivers required for the chosen route.
  • Draft terms, payment conditions, costs and realistic timing.
  • Final records to receive and check after completion.

Questions worth resolving early

Is a signed agreement or possession receipt enough?

Do not treat it as a substitute for confirming the holder’s rights, buyer eligibility and the required formalities. Ask what legal effect the specific document has and which registrations or other steps remain.

Can common-use land follow the parcel-sale process?

Common-use land has a different legal framework. Ask an agrarian attorney to identify the permitted route and required ejido decisions for the proposed arrangement; do not assume that an individual parcel-rights contract applies. [1]

Does a settlement lot always remain ejidal?

Check the actual title. Settlement lots (solares) can have their own title and private-law treatment under the Agrarian Law. A location inside an ejido does not settle the issue by itself. [1]

How long does conversion or a sale take?

There is no reliable single timetable for every property. Ask for a staged estimate identifying documents, assembly or registry steps, notices and unresolved issues. The guide does not promise conversion, approval or a closing date.

Can I develop once the title is resolved?

Ask for a separate review of land use, water, access, environmental requirements and the proposed development. Clear ownership paperwork alone should not be used as evidence that a project is authorized.

What should I send BIR first?

Send the location, approximate area, whether you are buying or selling and the names of the documents available. Start with a description of the situation; arrange how to share document copies with the people reviewing the matter.
